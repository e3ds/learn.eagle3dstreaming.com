How long a viewer waits before they can use your app, what that time is spent on, and what actually shortens it.

What to expect

SituationTypical wait
Preallocated — already runningUnder 10 seconds
Streamed often, so the app is warm on our machines10–20 seconds
First time it has been streamed20–30 seconds

These are ranges, not guarantees, and the variable is your app. Two applications on identical hardware load at different speeds because of what is in them — how much content, how it is structured, how much has to exist before the first frame. The platform's share of the wait is roughly fixed; yours is not.

What the wait is spent on

  1. Acquiring

    Finding a machine for the session.

  2. Preparing

    Getting your application onto that machine.

  3. Starting

    Your app launching — the part you control, and usually the largest part for anything substantial.

A preallocated app skips the first two entirely, because the app is already running when the viewer arrives. That is why it is the largest single improvement available and why it costs what it does.

Measuring it

Two ways, and the second is the one worth using.

  • By hand. Time from opening the URL to the app responding. Fine for a rough number.
  • In the browser's developer tools. Open them with F12, use the Network tab while the stream starts, and you get the phases separately rather than one total — which is the difference between knowing it is slow and knowing which part is slow.

Measure the packaged app on your own machine first. That is the floor. Streaming adds to it and cannot subtract from it, so if the native build takes forty seconds to reach its menu, no amount of platform tuning will produce a ten-second stream.

Shortening it

In rough order of effect:

  1. Load less before the first frame

    Start with a smaller map and bring in the rest afterwards. Unreal's level streaming loads only the parts of the world that are needed, which cuts both the wait and the memory the app holds.

  2. Load in the background

    Asynchronous loading lets the app become usable while the remaining assets arrive, rather than making the viewer wait for all of it.

  3. Preallocate, if the wait genuinely matters

    For a live demo or a public-facing experience, this removes the platform's share of the wait entirely. It costs a machine running continuously, so it is a trade rather than an improvement — both sides of it here.

  4. Show something while they wait

    This does not shorten the wait, and it changes how long it feels. A loading screen with your branding beats a blank frame, which reads as a broken stream.
